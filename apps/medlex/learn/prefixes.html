02 · The hook

The hook · two charts

Explore the detail panels, retrieve the meaning, then explain the distinction.

A chart mentions hyperglycaemia . Another says hypoglycaemia . Before you read anything else, what changed?

hyper- = above normal or excessive → hyperglycaemia refers to elevated blood glucose.

hypo- = below normal or deficient → hypoglycaemia refers to low blood glucose.

A prefix provides a clue, not a full diagnosis. Interpret the complete clinical term and its context.

A one-letter difference can reverse the direction of a concept.

08 · Time & sequence

Time · a clinical timeline

Explore the detail panels, retrieve the meaning, then explain the distinction.

Time-related prefixes orient a clinical phrase relative to an event. Click a moment to see it in use.

pre- before · preoperative

peri- around · perioperative

post- after · postoperative

retro- behind/backward · retroperitoneal

Notice that retro- describes a position/direction here, not necessarily a moment in time. Categories are learning aids, not rigid grammatical rules.

“Perioperative” is a context-specific term for the period around an operation; peri- means “around,” not “during” universally. [3]

10 · Common misreads

Confusable pairs

Explore the detail panels, retrieve the meaning, then explain the distinction.

These pairs look or sound related. Can you explain the distinction before you flip?

intercostal: between the ribs.

intra- = within, as in intravenous.

intravenous: within a vein.

inter- = between, as in intercostal.

epidermis: outer layer of the skin.

peri- = around, as in pericardium.

pericardium: the sac around the heart.

epi- = upon; similar-looking, different position.

apnoea: cessation of breathing.

an- is used before some vowels; spelling depends on the term.

dysphagia: difficulty swallowing.

Not simply “absence”; compare a- / an-.

hyperglycaemia: high blood glucose.

hypo- = below / deficient.

hypoglycaemia: low blood glucose.

hyper- = above / excessive.

Word-part decoding is a study strategy; established definitions take precedence. [2]

15 · Clinical literacy

Safety · context matters

Explore the detail panels, retrieve the meaning, then explain the distinction.

Word parts help you form a hypothesis about language. They do not confirm a diagnosis, a code or a treatment decision.

Established usage can be more specific than the literal sum of its pieces. Confirm what the complete term actually means.

A form such as in- can mean “not” in one word and “in/into” in another; para- can change nuance with context.

Patient findings, approved definitions and local clinical or coding standards take priority over word-part guessing.

Locate → decode → check the whole term → verify in context .

For education only; illustrations are conceptual, not diagnostic. [4]
