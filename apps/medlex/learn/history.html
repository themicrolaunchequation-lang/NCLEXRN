ERA 01 / MEDICAL RECORDS

Before print,
medicine was written.

Ancient documents preserve observation, remedies and ways of organising illness.

Edwin Smith Surgical Papyrus

A trauma text organised into 48 cases, including examination, assessment and approaches to treatment.

Broader traditions

Egyptian papyri and Mesopotamian medical texts show that record keeping and naming long predate modern terminology.

What survives

The habit of documenting a clinical problem has a long history; modern definitions should not be projected onto every ancient description.

ERA 02 / GREEK MEDICINE

A productive source
of word parts.

Greek texts and later borrowing contributed many roots used in modern medicine.

SOURCESNLM / Medical word partsNLM / Greek sources and Galen

ERA 03 / TRANSMISSION

Greek and Latin
travelled together.

The language of medicine grew through reuse, translation and teaching.

01

Celsus

De Medicina transmitted medical discussion in Latin.

02

Galen

A major Greek-language medical author whose works were translated and studied for centuries.

03

Two roots, one organ

Greek nephr/o and Latin ren/o both refer to the kidney. Nephrology and renal describe the same organ from different word families.

AVOID A RIGID SPLIT

Greek is often prominent in disease and specialty terms; Latin is prominent in anatomical naming. Neither language belongs exclusively to one category.

ERA 04 / ARABIC SCHOLARSHIP

Translation was
also innovation.

Medical knowledge changed as scholars compared, organised and extended earlier work.

Greek → Arabic

Translation made earlier medical writings available to new scholarly communities.

New synthesis

Authors such as al-Razi and Ibn Sina produced influential medical works; Arabic scholarship added knowledge rather than merely storing it.

Arabic → Latin

Latin translations of Ibn Sina’s Canon helped shape medical teaching in Europe.

SOURCESNLM / Medieval Islamic medicineNLM / Medieval manuscripts

ERA 05 / MEDIEVAL EUROPE

Books crossed borders.
So did their language.

Schools, libraries and translators connected several medical traditions.

01

Teaching centres

Salerno and other centres brought medical texts into teaching traditions.

02

Translation networks

Latin versions of Arabic and Greek works circulated through manuscript copying and, later, print.

03

Shared scholarly language

Latin supported communication between scholars, while terminology continued to change.

READ OLD TERMS IN CONTEXT

Historical words such as “dropsy” described clinical appearances. Do not automatically treat every old label as a single modern diagnosis.

SOURCESNLM / Medieval manuscriptsNLM / Medieval Islamic medicine

ERA 06 / RENAISSANCE

Look at the body.
Revisit the authority.

Vesalius’s Fabrica was published in Basel in 1543.

Observation

Vesalius performed detailed dissections and questioned parts of inherited anatomical teaching.

Print and illustration

The book’s images helped observations circulate beyond the dissection room.

A lasting lesson

Names and descriptions should be checked against evidence, rather than repeated only because they are familiar.

SOURCESNLM / Vesalius and the Fabrica

ERA 07 / SCIENTIFIC EXPANSION

New tools.
New distinctions.

Microscopy, experimental science and new procedures expanded the vocabulary.

Microscopy

Descriptions of cells and tissues required increasingly detailed language.

New techniques

Imaging and anaesthesia brought new terminology and new uses of older words.

A naming problem

Synonyms and local traditions could make anatomical communication difficult; shared standards became increasingly valuable.

ERA 08 / STANDARDISATION

One structure.
A shared reference.

Anatomical terminology is maintained through international collaboration.

01

1895 / Basle Nomina Anatomica

An important early international effort to reduce competing anatomical names.

02

1998 / Terminologia Anatomica

An international anatomical terminology published before the current second edition.

03

2019 / TA2

The second edition was published online in 2019 and approved by the IFAA General Assembly.

STANDARD DOES NOT MEAN ALL SYNONYMS DISAPPEAR

Descriptive names support clarity. Eponyms and local terms still occur, so learners need to recognise and verify them.

SOURCESIFAA / FIPAT / International anatomical terminology

ERA 09 / DIGITAL HEALTH

Names, concepts and codes
do different jobs.

Word analysis supports understanding; coding requires its own standards and rules.

01

Anatomical nomenclature

TA2 provides a shared framework for anatomical names.

02

Clinical terminology

SNOMED CT represents clinical meanings using concepts, descriptions and relationships.

03

Classification

ICD-11 groups health conditions for recording and reporting. It came into effect in 2022; country implementation follows different timelines.

DO NOT INFER A CODE FROM WORD PARTS

Documentation, purpose, jurisdiction and the applicable release determine coding. Morphology alone is not a coding rule.
